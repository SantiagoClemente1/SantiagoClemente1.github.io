TL;DR

We reviewed the recent research on how AI, wearables, and eye-tracking are used to measure athletic performance, with a focus on soccer and youth athletes. These tools now capture both physical and mental performance, but no study we found combined them with nutrition data, which is a key missing piece for understanding how young athletes grow and recover.

Why this review

Sports tech has exploded: GPS vests, heart-rate straps, smart textiles, and AI video analysis now give coaches data that didn't exist a decade ago. Most reviews focus on the physical side (speed, load, injury risk). We wanted to know how well these tools also capture the cognitive side (decision-making, attention, mental fatigue), especially for youth and amateur athletes, and where the gaps are.

How we did it

We followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews and searched SCOPUS and IEEE Xplore for peer-reviewed, English-language studies from January 2018 to August 2025.

Search string ("soccer" AND "player" AND "performance" AND "tracking") AND ("technology" OR "AI" OR "machine learning" OR "wearable" OR "sensor" OR "eye-tracking")

Studies had to track physical or cognitive performance using AI, wearables, sensors, or eye-tracking, and report real data. Each included study was quality-checked with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).

What we found

The 16 studies fell into four groups.

01

AI & machine learning

Computer vision models (YOLO, deepSORT) detect players and the ball in game footage automatically. Other models predict injury risk from heart-rate and sprint data, or detect cognitive load from an athlete's speech.

02

Wearables & sensors

GPS and heart-rate wearables quantify speed, acceleration, and training load. Newer work combines multiple sensors into a single performance index, or gives real-time resistance feedback to build sprint capacity.

03

Youth & amateur athletes

Tools are being adapted for younger players: age-specific injury thresholds, readiness scores from GPS data, and eye-tracking to study how young players scan the field and make decisions.

04

Eye-tracking & attention

The least explored area. Athletes show more target-focused gaze, and elite players track multiple moving objects better under pressure. Promising for coaching, but devices can be intrusive.

The gap: nutrition

None of the 16 studies included dietary or metabolic data. That's a big blind spot, because nutrition drives recovery, energy balance, and growth, especially for young athletes. Only a few studies combined more than one domain (for example, AI with physiology, or eye-tracking with decision-making), and small sample sizes and inconsistent measures make results hard to compare.

Where the field should go next

  • Add nutrition data to wearable and cognitive tracking for a full picture of performance and recovery.
  • Build youth-specific models instead of reusing ones trained on adult athletes.
  • Make devices work together so AI, wearable, and eye-tracking data can be combined in one place.
  • Run long-term studies on how early tracking affects skill development, resilience, and injury risk over years.
